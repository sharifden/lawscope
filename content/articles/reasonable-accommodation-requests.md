## Start with the setting

“Reasonable accommodation” appears in several areas of disability law, but the rules are not identical. Employment, housing, education, state and local government services, transportation, and public accommodations can involve different statutes, responsible organizations, definitions, procedures, and remedies.

Identify the setting before relying on a form or deadline. An employer’s process under the Americans with Disabilities Act differs from a housing provider’s duties under fair housing law, and state law may add protections.

## A request does not always need special words

In many settings, a person does not need to say “reasonable accommodation” or cite a statute. A communication may be enough if it explains that a change or assistance is needed because of a disability-related limitation. Still, a clear written request can reduce uncertainty about what was asked and when.

Identify the policy, practice, environment, schedule, communication method, or other barrier involved. Describe the requested change and how it would help address the limitation. A person does not necessarily need to disclose every detail of a diagnosis.

## Keep a reliable record

Save the request, attachments, delivery confirmation, and responses. After an oral conversation, a brief follow-up email can summarize the request and any agreed next steps. Record dates for meetings, requested documents, trial periods, and decisions.

Documentation should remain factual. It can identify functional limitations and the connection between those limitations and the requested change without including unrelated medical history.

## When supporting information may be requested

If a disability or need is not obvious, an organization may be allowed to request limited supporting information. The permissible scope depends on the setting and law. Requests for complete medical files may be broader than necessary.

A health professional’s letter may describe relevant limitations, expected duration, and how the accommodation would help. The organization should handle disability-related information under applicable confidentiality rules.

## Participate in the interactive process

Employment law often describes an interactive process in which the employer and employee discuss possible effective accommodations. Similar communication can be useful in other settings even where terminology differs.

The first requested option is not always the only effective option. Consider whether another schedule, device, communication method, policy adjustment, service, or location change would address the barrier. A temporary measure or trial period may help evaluate effectiveness.

## Reasons a request may be limited

The applicable law may allow denial when a requested change would cause undue hardship, impose an undue financial or administrative burden, fundamentally alter a program, create a qualifying direct threat, or fall outside the responsible party’s legal duties. These standards are fact-specific and differ by statute.

A bare statement that a request is “too difficult” may not explain the analysis. Ask for the decision and reason in writing, and ask whether an effective alternative is available.

## Deadlines and escalation

Internal review, grievance, union, agency, or court procedures may have different and sometimes short deadlines. Continuing informal discussions may not pause an external filing deadline.

Depending on the setting, help may be available from the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, the Department of Housing and Urban Development, a state or local civil-rights agency, a protection-and-advocacy organization, or a licensed attorney.

## A practical request outline

A useful written request can identify the person making it, the relevant barrier, the disability-related need, the requested change, supporting information if appropriate, and a preferred contact method. Keep a copy and continue documenting whether the response and any implemented accommodation are effective.
