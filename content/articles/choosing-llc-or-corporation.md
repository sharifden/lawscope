## Start with the business, not the label

A limited liability company and a corporation are legal structures created under state law. Both can separate business obligations from an owner’s personal affairs when properly formed and maintained, but they differ in governance, ownership, tax options, fundraising conventions, and administrative formality.

No structure is universally best. The right choice depends on the owners, the state, the type of business, financing plans, expected profits, tax circumstances, and tolerance for formal procedures. Legal and tax advice should address the actual plan.

## Ownership and management

An LLC is owned by one or more members. State law and an operating agreement can allow flexible management by members or designated managers. The agreement can address voting, profit allocations, transfers, departures, disputes, and dissolution.

A corporation is owned by shareholders. Shareholders elect directors, and directors oversee major decisions and appoint officers to manage daily operations. Corporate bylaws, resolutions, stock records, and meeting or consent procedures create a more standardized governance system.

That formality can be useful when ownership will change or outside investors expect familiar rights. A small owner-managed business may prefer the contractual flexibility of an LLC, but it still benefits from clear records and a thorough operating agreement.

## Liability protection has limits

LLCs and corporations are commonly chosen for limited-liability protection. In general, an owner is not personally responsible merely because the entity owes a debt. Protection is not absolute, however.

An owner can remain responsible for a personal guarantee, personal wrongdoing, unpaid obligations covered by a specific statute, or conduct that justifies disregarding the entity under state law. Mixing personal and business funds, misleading creditors, or failing to maintain the entity can create risk.

Separate bank accounts, accurate records, adequate capitalization, proper signatures, contracts in the entity’s name, insurance, and compliance with state requirements all support the separation.

## Federal tax classification

Legal form and federal tax classification are related but not identical. A single-member LLC is generally treated as disregarded for federal income tax unless an election is made. A multi-member LLC is generally treated as a partnership unless it elects corporate treatment.

A corporation is generally taxed under the corporate rules. A qualifying corporation may elect S corporation treatment if it meets federal requirements. An LLC that meets the requirements may also elect to be taxed as an S corporation. The election does not turn the LLC into a corporation under state organizational law.

Payroll, self-employment tax, reasonable compensation, distributions, losses, benefits, and state taxes can change the result. Tax outcomes should be modeled rather than chosen from a slogan.

## Funding and ownership changes

Traditional venture-capital investors often prefer a corporation because stock classes, equity incentives, governance rights, and future financing rounds fit established practices. Some investors have tax or policy reasons not to invest in pass-through entities.

An LLC can still raise capital, admit new members, and create different economic rights, but the operating agreement and tax consequences may become complex. Businesses planning broad employee equity or an eventual public offering commonly consider a corporation early.

For a closely held consulting, property, or family business, LLC flexibility may be more useful. Transfer restrictions and buyout terms should be addressed before a disagreement or unexpected departure occurs.

## Ongoing compliance

Both structures require formation filings and ongoing state compliance. Costs can include registered-agent service, annual or periodic reports, franchise taxes, local licenses, and industry permits. The amounts and deadlines differ by state.

Corporations generally use more formal governance records. LLC requirements may be less rigid, but “less formal” should not mean undocumented. Written approvals, ownership records, financial statements, and current agreements can prevent uncertainty.

## The practical takeaway

The decision is a combination of state law, tax planning, governance, financing, and risk management. Owners can compare formation and annual costs, desired management rights, investor expectations, compensation plans, tax projections, and exit goals. A business lawyer and tax professional can then test the structure against the actual facts before documents are filed.
