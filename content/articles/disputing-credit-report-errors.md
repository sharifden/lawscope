## Review reports carefully

A credit report can affect lending, housing, insurance, and other decisions. Common concerns include accounts that do not belong to the consumer, incorrect balances, duplicate debts, inaccurate payment history, or information that should no longer appear under applicable rules.

Review the report from each nationwide credit reporting company because their files may differ. Save a complete copy showing the date it was obtained and mark each disputed item without altering the original record.

## Distinguish an error from a disagreement

A dispute process addresses inaccurate or incomplete information. A negative item is not necessarily inaccurate simply because it harms a score or relates to a difficult event. Identify the specific field or account detail believed to be wrong and the factual reason.

If identity theft may be involved, additional blocking, fraud-alert, security-freeze, and reporting procedures may apply. Use official government and credit-reporting-company channels rather than links in unexpected messages.

## Gather focused supporting records

Useful documents may include account statements, payment confirmations, correspondence, identity-theft reports, court records, cancellation notices, or proof that an [account belongs to someone else](/categories/legal-basics/). Send copies rather than irreplaceable originals and redact unrelated sensitive information when appropriate.

Create a short list matching each disputed entry to the supporting document. A focused explanation can make the requested correction easier to understand.

## Contact the reporting company

A consumer can generally dispute information with the credit reporting company that produced the report. The company’s official instructions explain available online, mail, or telephone methods and what identifying information is required.

Describe the item, explain why it is inaccurate or incomplete, state the requested correction, and include supporting copies. Keep the confirmation number, submission date, full dispute packet, and proof of mailing or upload.

## Contact the information furnisher

The bank, lender, collector, landlord, or other business that supplied the information is often called a furnisher. A consumer may also send a dispute to the furnisher using its designated address or process.

The report or account correspondence may identify the furnisher. Avoid sending sensitive documents to an address that has not been verified through a reliable source.

## Follow the investigation

Federal law generally requires an investigation within a defined period, subject to exceptions and extensions. The exact timing can depend on how the report was obtained, whether additional information is submitted, and whether a dispute is considered frivolous or irrelevant.

Review the written results and any updated report. Confirm whether the specific fields changed across each relevant reporting company. A correction at one company may not automatically update another.

## If the problem remains

A consumer can consider sending additional material that directly addresses the stated result, asking that a statement of dispute be included where available, submitting a complaint to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, or consulting a consumer-law attorney.

Do not repeatedly submit identical disputes without new information merely to restart the process. Preserve a chronology of reports, submissions, delivery records, responses, and any concrete harm caused by the disputed information.

## Protect personal information

Credit disputes require sensitive identifiers. Use secure official websites, verify mailing addresses, avoid public Wi-Fi for uploads, and retain documents in protected storage. A legitimate dispute does not require payment to a credit-repair company, and no company can lawfully guarantee removal of accurate negative information.
