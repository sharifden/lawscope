## Why early records matter

An injury claim can turn on facts that become harder to reconstruct with time. Physical conditions change, surveillance footage may be overwritten, witnesses move, and memories fade. Preserving information does not prove negligence or guarantee compensation, but it can help create a more accurate record.

Evidence rules, limitation periods, notice requirements, and available damages differ by jurisdiction and claim type. Claims involving a government body, workplace injury, defective product, medical care, or a minor may have special procedures.

## Address health and safety first

Immediate health and safety should take priority over gathering evidence. A person should seek appropriate medical attention and follow emergency instructions. Medical records created during ordinary care may later document symptoms, diagnoses, treatment, restrictions, and follow-up recommendations.

Describe symptoms accurately without exaggeration or minimization. Keep appointment summaries, discharge instructions, prescriptions, and referrals. If a condition changes, communicate with an appropriate health professional rather than relying only on a personal journal.

## Photograph changing conditions

When it is safe and lawful, photographs or video may preserve the scene, lighting, weather, property condition, warning signs, vehicle position, damaged items, and visible injuries. Wide views can provide context while closer images show detail.

Retain the original files when possible. Editing, filtering, or repeatedly resaving an image can remove metadata or invite questions about alteration. Back up files securely and record the approximate date, time, location, and photographer.

## Identify records and witnesses

Write down the names and contact information of people who directly observed the event or its immediate aftermath. Avoid pressuring witnesses or suggesting what they should say. [Incident reports](/articles/what-happens-after-an-arrest/), emergency call records, inspection logs, maintenance records, receipts, and photographs held by another party may also [matter](/categories/personal-injury/).

Some records are routinely deleted. A lawyer may advise whether a prompt preservation notice is appropriate and who should receive it. A preservation request should be accurate and targeted; it is not a substitute for formal discovery.

## Keep communications intact

Save relevant emails, letters, text messages, claim correspondence, and voicemail. Preserve the full conversation rather than isolated excerpts. Record the date, name, organization, and substance of important telephone calls.

Be cautious about public social-media posts. Even a post intended for friends may be copied or interpreted without context. Do not delete information to conceal it once a dispute is anticipated; ask counsel about lawful preservation obligations.

## Track financial effects

Keep invoices, receipts, mileage logs, wage records, benefit statements, and documentation of medically related equipment or assistance. A simple chronological log can connect expenses with dates and purposes. Not every expense is legally recoverable, but missing records can make an otherwise valid item difficult to verify.

An employer may be able to provide attendance, leave, schedule, and wage information. Self-employed people may need business records showing historical income and the basis for claimed losses.

## Protect originals and privacy

Store originals in a secure location and work from copies. Medical, employment, insurance, and identification records can contain sensitive information, so use secure sharing methods and send only what is reasonably necessary.

Before signing a broad authorization, recorded statement, settlement, or release, understand its scope and consequences. A licensed attorney can identify deadlines, required notices, preservation duties, and claim-specific evidence under the controlling law.
