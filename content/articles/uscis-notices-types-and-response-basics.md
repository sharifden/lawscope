## Start with the exact notice

U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services sends notices for different procedural reasons. The form number, receipt number, notice date, case type, response address, and deadline can affect what happens next. A person should read the entire notice rather than relying on a generic description of its title.

USCIS forms, filing addresses, fees, and policies can change. Verify instructions through the official USCIS website and the edition of the form identified for the case. Immigration consequences can be significant, so individualized advice may be appropriate.

## Receipt and account notices

A receipt notice generally confirms that USCIS accepted a filing for processing and assigned a receipt number. Acceptance does not necessarily mean the requested benefit was approved or that every eligibility issue has been resolved.

Check that the name, address, classification, and filing type appear correct. Store the original securely and use the receipt number only through official channels. Certain receipt notices may contain language about temporary evidence or an extension, but the exact printed terms control.

## Appointment notices

USCIS may schedule biometrics, an interview, or another appointment. The notice typically states the date, time, location, identification requirements, and rescheduling procedure. Missing an appointment can delay or harm a case.

If attendance is impossible, follow the notice’s official instructions promptly. Do not assume that a telephone call, informal message, or change in an online calendar completed a rescheduling request.

## Requests for Evidence

A Request for Evidence, often called an RFE, states that USCIS needs specified information before deciding a filing. The response deadline and submission location appear on the notice. USCIS may expect one organized response containing all requested items.

Compare each request with the original filing and the applicable eligibility requirement. Include a copy of the notice when directed, use a clear index or cover letter where helpful, and retain a complete copy plus delivery proof. Submitting unrelated material can obscure rather than answer the request.

## Notices of Intent

A Notice of Intent to Deny, Revoke, Rescind, or Terminate signals a potentially adverse action and gives the recipient an opportunity to respond under the stated procedure. These notices can raise legal or factual issues that differ from a routine evidence request.

Because the consequences may be serious, consider prompt help from a [licensed immigration attorney](/categories/legal-basics/) or an accredited representative recognized by the U.S. Department of Justice. Avoid unlicensed “notarios” or consultants who are not authorized to provide legal advice.

## Transfer, approval, and denial notices

A transfer notice usually says that another office will handle the case. An approval notice identifies the approved request and effective terms, but it does not always serve as every document needed for travel, work, or status verification.

A denial notice should state reasons and may describe a motion, appeal, or other option. Deadlines can be short and different rules may apply to filing dates and delivery. A denial should not be ignored even if another application is pending.

## Address changes and fraud awareness

Keep USCIS informed of an address change through the required official process; postal forwarding alone may not update agency records. [Preserve confirmation of the change](/articles/preserving-evidence-after-an-injury/) and continue monitoring case communications.

USCIS notices should not direct payment through gift cards, cryptocurrency, or informal transfers. Verify suspicious communications using official USCIS contact tools rather than contact details supplied only by the suspicious message.

## A careful response process

Create a deadline calendar, keep the envelope and notice, preserve a complete response copy, and use trackable delivery when permitted. The safest response depends on the exact filing history, immigration status, notice language, and current law—not merely the notice’s general title.
