## Confirm the document

Before interpreting a court decision, identify the [court](/categories/legal-basics/), date, case name, citation or docket number, and document type. A majority opinion, [order](/articles/child-custody-orders-common-factors/), dissent, concurrence, trial-court ruling, and party brief do not carry the same meaning or authority.

Use an official court source when available. Online summaries and headlines can help locate a case, but they may omit procedural details, later corrections, or limits in the opinion itself.

## Place the court in the hierarchy

Authority depends partly on which court issued the decision and which jurisdiction’s law applies. A higher court’s holding may bind lower courts within its system. A decision from another jurisdiction may be persuasive rather than binding. Trial-court rulings usually have a different reach from appellate precedent.

Federal and state systems also interact. A federal court may decide a question of state law, and a state court may address federal constitutional or statutory issues. Identify the source of the rule being interpreted.

## Read the procedural history

The procedural section explains how the dispute reached the current court. It may identify a motion to dismiss, summary judgment, trial verdict, agency decision, injunction, or earlier appeal.

Procedure shapes what the court could decide and the standard it applied. For example, a court reviewing dismissal may assume properly pleaded facts for a limited purpose without finding those facts true after a trial.

## Separate facts from allegations

Opinions often summarize facts, but the status of those facts matters. Some may be allegations, stipulated facts, jury findings, or evidence viewed in favor of one party under a particular review standard.

Look for signal phrases such as “the complaint alleges,” “the jury found,” or “viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to.” Avoid repeating an allegation as an established fact.

## State the legal issue narrowly

The issue is the legal question the court needed to answer, not the entire social or political controversy around the case. Frame it with the relevant law, procedural posture, and material facts.

A narrow issue statement makes it easier to distinguish what the court actually resolved from topics it discussed but did not decide.

## Find the holding

The holding is the rule or conclusion necessary to the judgment. Courts may announce it explicitly, but readers sometimes need to connect the legal standard with the outcome. Broad commentary not necessary to the result may be considered dicta and may carry less precedential weight.

Check the disposition near the end: affirmed, reversed, vacated, dismissed, or remanded. A remand sends further proceedings back to a lower court and may not end the case.

## Trace the reasoning

Identify the legal authorities the court used, the test or standard it applied, and how it connected the facts to that standard. Note any limiting language, exceptions, unresolved questions, or dependence on a particular record.

Separate opinions matter. A concurrence agrees with the judgment but may use different reasoning. A dissent disagrees and is not the majority holding, though it can influence later debate. A plurality opinion may require additional analysis to identify the controlling rule.

## Check what happened later

A decision may be amended, withdrawn, reheard, appealed, stayed, limited, or overruled. Verify subsequent history and current validity before relying on it. Publication status and citation rules also differ across courts.

## Build a concise case brief

A practical summary can list the court and date, relevant facts, procedural posture, issue, rule, holding, reasoning, disposition, and important limits. Link to the official opinion and clearly label any analysis. For decisions affecting an actual legal matter, current research and advice from a licensed attorney may be necessary.
